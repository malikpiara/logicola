# The voice standard — Gensler's own, from primary sources

Written 2026-08-24 for the site-wide copy review, after Malik's note
that "will reach you when it ships" reads as software-industry
language rather than something written for a professor or a student.

Everything quoted here is Gensler's, recovered from the 2008 program
itself and transcribed: the help screens
are stored encoded and the UI strings sit plain in the original program. This is
not an impression of his style; it is his text.

## What he sounds like

**He talks to one student, in the second person, about what to do
next.**

> You test an English syllogism by first translating it into symbols
> (using the same letter for the same idea and different letters for
> different ideas)

> When you do an English argument, first try to give an intuitive
> appraisal of whether the argument is valid or invalid; then work it
> out in symbols

> Highlight the exercise set you want to do.

**He hedges advice as advice, not as a rule.** The concession is the
teacherly move — it grants that the shortcut comes later:

> The first few times, you might want to underline all the distributed
> letters — and then star premise letters that are underlined

**He warns plainly, without alarm:**

> Be careful about whether you use a small or a capital letter.

> Be sure to capitalize correctly.

**The program speaks as a person, in the first person:**

> Before we can do logic, I need to get your name.

**Praise is short, warm, and unexcited.** The whole vocabulary is:

> Congratulations on completing the exercise! · Good work, ·
> Excellent · Good response · Sorry, try again.

**"We" means the discipline, not the company:**

> we use capital letters for general categories

> These vocabulary items can combine to form … English has various
> idiomatic ways to express our wffs.

**His own release notes are past-tense and honest about cost:**

> Oct 22, 2013: Fixed some display problems in Windows 8.1 (but at the
> cost of additional flickering during the problems).

> Apr 14, 2012: Improved the directions for sending scores by email
> and fixed a proofs bug.

> Dec 1-3, 2011: Added Cloud Sync and improved the display of scores
> on LogiSkor.

## The rules that fall out of it

1. **Address the student, not the market.** "You" and an imperative
   beat a feature claim. He never sells the program inside the
   program.
2. **No industry verbs.** He never ships, launches, drops, unlocks or
   levels up. Things are _added_, _fixed_, _improved_, _available_.
   New work "is here" or "is ready"; it does not "ship".
3. **Say what to do next.** Nearly every sentence of his ends with an
   action the student can take.
4. **Understate praise.** "Good work" carries more weight in his
   register than any superlative would. Reserve exclamation for the
   end of a whole exercise, which is the one place he uses it.
5. **Plain words for plain things; technical words only for the
   subject.** `wff`, `distributed`, `valid` are precise and stay.
   "Seamless", "powerful", "effortless" have no equivalent in his
   text.
6. **Concede honestly.** The Windows 8.1 note names the cost of its
   own fix. An admission of a trade-off reads as trustworthy to an
   instructor deciding whether to assign this.
7. **Contractions are fine** ("doesn't", "you'll", "don't") — he is
   informal, not stiff. The register is a good teacher talking, not a
   textbook.

## What this rules out on our side

Marketing energy ("Master Formal Logic" is a claim about the reader's
outcome, not an instruction), software-release framing ("when it
ships", "what's new"), and any sentence whose subject is the product
rather than the student or the logic.
