Two marks, split by size — can it be done?

Yes, and the mechanism is already in place. Malik, 2026-08-23: “the wordmark for the big use cases and the favicon can for the 2 small ones.” The favicon and the installed-app icons are declared through two independent channels, so pointing them at different artwork needs no trickery — it is the ordinary arrangement, and it is what most brands with a lettering mark actually ship.

One measurement had to be redone for this to be true. Android crops a maskable icon to a circle of 40% of the canvas, so the artwork’s furthest CORNER — not its edge — must land inside a 204.8px radius on a 512 tile. The can’s corner sits at 227.3px and needs a 0.901 shrink; the wordmark is 1.4:1 wide, its corner sits at 233.1px and it needs 0.879. Reusing the can’s number for the wordmark would have clipped the L and the a. Both fits are now computed per mark, so the maskable files below are correct for whichever mark wins.

Three things to decide with it, none of them blocking. (1) useQuizFavicon repaints app/icon.svg in each set’s tab colour for the duration of a run — so the small mark is already per-set, and whatever pair the can takes is only its resting state. (2) The two marks should share a colour pair, or the tab and the home screen stop reading as the same product; that is the whole cost of the split and it is a design cost, not a technical one. (3) There is no apple-icon at all today, so iOS currently installs a screenshot of the page — the split fixes that as a side effect rather than causing it.

The folklore that rules out a wordmark icon is mostly folklore

A research round with an adversarial check on every claim went after “never put type in an app icon”, and most of it did not survive:

  • Microsoft does not prohibit typography. It says letters “should be avoided and only used when it’s essential” — the carve-out is in the same sentence — and Microsoft ships Word, Excel and PowerPoint as letter plates through the October 2025 redesign.
  • Apple does not cap icon text at a single letter. The first-letter example is illustrative; the prohibition is on nonessential words. A wordmark that IS the brand is licensed by the opening sentence.
  • Apple has no stroke-weight rule about fine features dissolving. Its stroke guidance is about consistency with adjacent text; the simplicity advice is advisory and qualified “at lower resolutions”.
  • No controlled study shows wordmark icons underperform pictorial ones on recognition.

So the case against the wordmark is not guidance. It is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is enough. The wordmark sits at 74% of a 512px plate, which is a scale of 1.43 against its 265-unit viewBox; at a 16px favicon that is 0.045 device pixels per viewBox unit, so a ~10-unit letter stroke renders at 0.45 of one device pixel. The bubbles, at 25 units in a 512 tile, land at 0.78px at the same size. Sub-pixel features do not render — that is the whole argument, and it applies to exactly one size.

Which points at a size-tiered family rather than a single winner: the wordmark or the bubbled can where the canvas can hold them (512, 180), the plain favicon can at 32 and 16. One silhouette family, detail dropped as the canvas shrinks. It costs nothing here because all three are already generated, and it is what brands with a lettering mark generally do.

Two things to settle before any of this ships, neither of which this sheet tests: dark mode, Safari’s monochrome pinned tab, and iOS tinted/clear icon variants — a two-colour knockout mark can collapse when the system flattens it to one ink — and the manifest split below.

Two whole families died on measurement, which is the useful part

Tab-colour grounds fail. Every “set tab colour as the ground” variant lands at 2.6–4.3:1 against a mint or ink can. That is not bad luck: the tab colours were engineered to sit at the balance point where they clear ~3.5:1 against both a light and a dark browser chrome. Being deliberately mid-value is exactly what disqualifies them as an icon ground, where you want one extreme, not the middle. They are still the right answer for the footer band — see docs/footer-band-lab.html — because there they are confetti on a ground, not the ground.

Ink ground + that set’s own accent fails. 1.5–3.0:1 across all seven. The harmony method predicts it before you render anything: pick accents for value contrast against the ink, not the background — two darks fight. Set R’s ink and accent are 1.54:1 apart, which is invisible.

What survives is one structure: a dark chromatic ground with a pale can. Every variant above 9:1 has that shape. It is also the right shape for the medium — a dark ground holds its edge on a light dock, a dark dock and a photo wallpaper, where today’s pale bright-green ground dissolves into the first of those.

Inverting, precisely

“Invert the colours” has two readings and both are in the field. The literal one keeps today’s two colours and swaps their roles — Today, literally inverted and its three darker-ground siblings. The scheme one re-lays the mark in Set L. They are not rivals: the literal inversion keeps the green heritage (LogiCola has been green since the Windows 3.1 build shipped a scheme named “Forest green”), and the scheme inversion matches the product a tap on the icon opens.

Worth noticing that Today inverted, dark end pulled to brand green scores 0.870 at 11.10:1 with a hue distance of 0.08 — a near-monochrome pair. The model marks it slightly calm (stim 0.47) rather than wrong. It is the most conservative real option on the sheet: nobody would say the brand changed colour, and yet it reads as a 2026 icon rather than a 2019 one.

Which mark — and the bubbles verdict

The coloured bubbles are out (Malik’s call, and the 16px row shows why): twelve 25px knockouts in a 512px tile are sub-pixel by the time the icon is a favicon, so the variant degrades to the plain pair exactly where an icon is working hardest. Family 7 and the coloured half of family 8 are kept as evidence, not as proposals.

That leaves a straight three-way comparison, and the three marks fail in different directions:

  • The shipped can carries the most character at 512px and loses its bubbles first. Its silhouette survives 16px because the outline is a single closed shape.
  • The favicon can is the most robust at 16px — no internal detail to lose — and the least distinctive at 512px. Look at it small before deciding: stripped of the bubbles and the clasp, a tall rounded rectangle with two ribs is a shape a lot of apps already own.
  • The wordmark is unmistakable at 104px and unreadable at 16px, which is the classic wordmark trade. It is also the mark the DECIDED avatar already uses, so choosing it would make the app icon and the social avatar the same object rather than siblings.

The brand’s own division of labour bears on this: the avatar carries the logo, the cover carries the pattern, neither ever does both (brand-decisions.md, 2026-08-12). An app icon is an avatar by that logic, which is an argument FOR the wordmark. The counter-argument is purely the 16px row, and it is not small — useQuizFavicon repaints app/icon.svg per set during a run, so the 16px case is not incidental to this product, it is a feature it already ships.

The honest resolution may be that these are two jobs: the wordmark at 512/180 where it reads, and a can at 16px where it must. That is what most brands with a lettering mark actually do, and it costs nothing here because both are already generated.

All seven sets in one mark (retained as evidence)

The twelve bubbles are separately addressable, so they can carry the palette instead of the ground. Seven sets — tab colours in the bubbles puts the whole product in the icon: the can is the app, the bubbles are the drills, and the colours are the ones a student already associates with each set. It costs nothing in contrast, because the can and ground pair is unchanged underneath — the score is identical to plain Set L.

Its real cost is scale. At 512px it is the most characterful thing here; at 16px the bubbles are sub-pixel and it degrades to the plain mark. That is not a defect — it is the classic responsive icon problem, and the honest fix is to ship the seven-bubble version at 512/180 and the plain pair as the favicon. The 16px row above is where to judge whether the degradation is graceful.

The orientation question, and the number that decides how to think about it

The decided avatar is a plum mark on a mint ground — a pale ground with a dark mark, which is the same orientation today’s app icon already has. So “invert the colours” moves the icon away from the avatar, not toward it. That is a real tension and it is Malik’s to resolve, but here is the measurement that makes it decidable rather than a matter of taste.

The can is 30.5% of the tile. The ground is 69.5%. Whatever colour the ground is, that is what the icon reads as from across a home screen — the mark is a detail inside it. So the orientation choice is not “which colour is the star”; it is “is this app a pale tile or a dark tile?”, and the honest answers differ by medium:

  • The avatar sits on a white LinkedIn or X page, inside a white ring the platform draws for it. A pale mint ground there is a deliberate softness and the ring holds the edge.
  • The app icon sits in a dock over an arbitrary wallpaper and on a home screen among saturated neighbours, with no ring. A 69.5% pale ground has nothing holding its edge on a light dock — the macOS dock · light strip above is where to check that claim rather than take it.

The brand lab already licenses treating them differently, in the same breath as it dropped the quilt’s ground gate for covers: a rule earns its authority from the job it was written for. Two artefacts, two jobs, one palette.

Recommendation — and it changed once the social decision landed

The cover in miniature (family 8) is the answer if coherence is the goal, and it is the only candidate that rhymes with both social artefacts at once. The cover is a mint ground carrying plum shapes and nine accent colours; this icon is a mint ground carrying one plum shape with those same accents knocked into it. It is the cover’s colour logic at icon scale, and it keeps the avatar’s orientation for free. It costs nothing in contrast — 12.67:1, identical to plain Set L — because the pair underneath is unchanged.

The cover inverted is the answer if the medium argument wins: same nine colours, same family, mint can on a plum ground, so it is a dark tile that holds its edge anywhere. It reads as a sibling of the covers rather than a copy of them.

Those two are the real choice. Everything else on this sheet is either evidence for how they were arrived at (the sets, the cross-set pairs, the two dead families) or a heritage option — Today inverted, dark end pulled to brand green, which is the one candidate that changes no brand colour at all and only swaps figure for ground. If the answer to “has the brand changed colour?” needs to be “no”, that is the one.

Whichever wins, note that the bubbles-carry-the-palette variants degrade to the plain pair at 16px, so the honest shipping split is the coloured version at 512/180 and the plain pair as the favicon.

How the ranking is computed

Per pair: perceptual intensity σ (HSL saturation corrected by a 30°-segment hue weight and eaten by extreme lightness), ΔL in CIELAB, circular hue distance θ, then stimulation = (4σ + 2ΔL + θ)/7. Vibration risk is 1 − √((1−σ)² + (1−θ)² + (1−vL)²) with vL falling to zero by ΔL 0.5. The composite weights contrast 0.42, distance from the mid-stimulation target 0.26, absence of vibration 0.14, shared temperature 0.10, and a non-muddy halfway blend 0.08 — contrast dominating because a 16px favicon is the binding case.

One caveat on reading the table: the model is orientation-blind. “Mint on plum” and “plum on mint” score identically because they are the same pair. Which colour is the ground is a separate judgement, made above on the medium: dark ground wins.
